The honest cost. The slope chart drops what the row carried — age, value, tier, the named neighbours. It answers a group question and cannot answer a player question, so it does not replace the row; it is what belongs above it. It also only works because both boards rank the same 337 shared players; on any other pair of lists the two scales would not be the same measurement.

Methodology — what 014 actually led with

Where the two boards agree

23 of your 27 players can be compared at all. Both boards are ranked over only the 337 players they share, because a rank from a list of 468 and a rank from a list of 399 are not the same measurement. Of the 12 receivers you hold, 0 are inside that starting 24 on our board and 2 on the market's.

A claim — the pattern the reference site uses on every figure

The market starts Burden every week. Our board has him on the bench.

Luther Burden is WR17 on the market — inside the 24 who start somewhere every week — and WR53 on ours, 29 places outside it. Rome Odunze also crosses, but sits on the line at WR24 — one rank of market movement and the crossing is gone. That is why the line is drawn and not asserted. The direction of the room as a whole is not the finding: the market is higher on 7 of your 12, which is 58% against a 56% league baseline for receivers this young. That is our model's age preference, not a fact about your room.

Each line joins one player's two ranks. Left · our board. Right · the market's. The dashed rule is the 24 receivers who start weekly league-wide. Highlighted: the players who cross that rule — inside it on one board, outside it on the other.

The test that separates them. Ask of each sentence: could the product be wrong about this? The metric label cannot be wrong — it names an axis. The methodology cannot be wrong — it counts things that are what they are. The claim can be wrong, and that is the whole of its value: it is the only one that spends the model's opinion, which is the only thing here that no other product has.
