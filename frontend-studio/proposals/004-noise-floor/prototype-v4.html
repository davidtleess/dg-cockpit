What this surface claims, and what it doesn't

The move is not the point. A price moves because someone had a big week, or the back ahead of him got hurt, or he won a job — you will hear that news before this screen tells you. What this screen knows that the news doesn't is where that move left the market relative to our model. That comparison is the only thing here you cannot get somewhere else, so it leads.

Reading a row. The blue mark is our percentile for that player among the modelled population; the amber mark is the market's. The shaded span between them is the disagreement. The faint amber ghost is where the market sat yesterday, so the arrow direction tells you whether today's move carried the market toward our view or further from it.

The noise filter. Cheap players' prices swing about 21% on an ordinary day; blue chips swing about 0.7% — a 28× spread measured across players and 28 days of capture. So "today's move" is reported as a multiple of that player's own ordinary day, and the unusual price move sort uses it. It decides what is worth printing; it is not itself the finding.

Not a recommendation, and not a claim that we are right. A gap between our percentile and the market's is a description of two numbers. It does not say the market is wrong, and nothing here says to trade anyone. Our model may be behind news the market has already priced — in the off-season it usually is.

Colour. Blue and amber are the app's existing model and market tokens, kept so the encoding means the same thing here as everywhere else. Checked with a contrast validator: colour-blind separation ΔE 22.5 (protan) and 22.8 (tritan) against a threshold of 8, normal-vision ΔE 24.3, and both clear 3:1 against the surface. Whether we or the market are higher is also carried by which side of the zero line the band sits on, so the reading never depends on colour alone.

Honest limits. Our percentile did not change for a single player today, so every gap movement on this screen is the market moving against a fixed model position. The comparison covers of priced players — the rest have no model percentile and are held out rather than shown as agreeing. Divergence history runs days, shorter than the 28-day price capture. Thresholds for "unusual" are borrowed from retail-market convention, not calibrated against outcomes; the realized-outcome loop is inactive until September.
